Chinese tea can look intimidating: hundreds of names, unfamiliar characters, gaiwans, pu-erh pressed into cakes. In practice it's simpler. There are six big families of tea, and to find yours it's enough to try one from a few of them.

## The six families in brief

| Family | Taste | You'll like it if |
|---|---|---|
| Green | fresh, grassy | you like light drinks |
| White | delicate, honeyed, floral | you like a soft taste |
| Oolong | from floral to roasted | almost everyone – the best start |
| Red | sweet, bready, honeyed | you're used to black tea |
| Sheng pu-erh | bright, fresh, a little astringent | you like bold flavours |
| Shou pu-erh | deep, earthy, full-bodied | you like coffee and cocoa |

Yellow tea exists too, but it's rare and not where you need to start.

## Where to start

### Light oolong

The most common "first tea" among our guests. Floral, creamy, barely bitter and forgiving if you get the brewing wrong. The classic is Tie Guanyin.

### Red tea

If you're used to black tea, start with red: what the West calls black tea, China calls red. Smooth, sweet and good without sugar. Thai wild-tree red tea is an interesting option.

### White tea

For people who like delicate flavours. Baihao Yinzhen buds are soft and honeyed; aged white tea pressed into cakes is deeper, with dried-fruit notes.

### Dark oolong

Roasted, with notes of nuts, chocolate and wood. Da Hong Pao is the best-known example and a good pick if you like a fuller taste.

### Pu-erh

Pu-erh is a world of its own. Shou is dark, smooth and earthy; sheng is bright, lively and changes with age. The difference in detail: [sheng vs shou pu-erh](/blog/sheng-vs-shou-pu-erh/).

## If you don't know what you like

Pick three teas from different families – say a light oolong, a red tea and a shou pu-erh. Taste them side by side and you'll know which way to go next.

## What teaware you need

One piece is enough to start:

- **Gaiwan** – a lidded cup that makes it easy to brew any tea in short infusions.
- **Cups** – one or two.
- **Fairness pitcher (cha hai)** – you pour the tea into it so it doesn't over-steep. Optional, but handy.

A travel set is a good choice if you want everything at once in a compact box.

## How to brew

Chinese tea is usually brewed gong fu style: lots of leaf, little water and short steeps of 10–30 seconds, repeated 5–10 times. Each infusion tastes a little different. Step by step: [how to brew oolong at home](/blog/how-to-brew-oolong/).

If gong fu feels like too much, brew it the usual way in a teapot: 3–5 g per 300 ml of water for 2–3 minutes.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Boiling water for green and white tea.** They prefer 75–85°C, otherwise they turn bitter.
- **Steeping too long.** Chinese tea is better brewed briefly, several times.
- **Buying a lot of one tea straight away.** Start with small packs of different teas.
- **Tea from souvenir shops.** Flavoured blends give the wrong idea of real tea. More in [where to buy real tea in Phuket](/blog/where-to-buy-real-tea-in-phuket/).
