Tea on a menu is often an afterthought: a box of bags and a kettle. Yet guests notice good tea, and it fits places where coffee doesn't – after a late dinner, at a spa, in the heat of a Phuket afternoon. Here is how to choose tea that works for your venue, step by step.

## Start from your guests and your service

Before choosing teas, answer a few questions. Who are your guests, and when do they drink tea – at breakfast, after a meal, all day long? Is it table service, a counter, or takeaway? Do you need hot tea, iced tea, or both? The answers decide everything else: the teas, the teaware and how the team will brew.

## Keep the first tea list short

A compact list is easier for guests to choose from and for the team to brew well. A good start is around five teas with clearly different characters:

- **A green or white tea** – light and fresh.
- **An oolong** – floral or roasted, depending on your food.
- **A red tea** (what the West calls black tea) – warm and familiar.
- **A shou pu-erh** – dark, smooth, good for evenings.
- **A caffeine-free herbal blend** – for late hours and children.

Add matcha or an iced tea if drinks are an important part of your menu. You can always grow the list once you see what guests order.

## Pair tea with your food

Pairing follows the same logic as wine: match intensity, then look for contrast or harmony.

| Tea | Works well with |
|---|---|
| Green and white tea | Delicate dishes, seafood, salads, fresh fruit |
| Light oolong | Asian dishes with herbs, light desserts |
| Roasted oolong and red tea | Grilled dishes, pastries, chocolate |
| Shou pu-erh | Rich and fatty dishes, long dinners |
| Herbal blends | Evenings, desserts, guests avoiding caffeine |

These are starting points – the best pairings come from tasting your own dishes with the teas.

## Decide how the tea will be brewed

A teapot at the table, a gaiwan for a small tea ceremony, or larger batches for iced tea – each needs different teaware, water temperature and timing. Whatever you choose, write it down. A **recipe card** for every tea – leaf weight, water volume, temperature and time – is what makes the tenth cup taste like the first.

## Train the team

The same tea can taste very different from two people's hands. A short training session on brewing, serving and talking about the tea gives staff confidence and gives guests a consistent cup.

## Store tea properly

Tea absorbs moisture and smells easily, and Phuket is humid. Keep it in airtight containers, away from light, heat and strong odours – not next to the spice shelf or the coffee grinder. Open stock in small amounts and close it right after use.

## Count the real cost of a cup

The leaf is only part of the cost. A fair calculation includes the tea, water, the team's time, teaware, breakage and leftovers. Base it on your recipe cards, not on guesses.
