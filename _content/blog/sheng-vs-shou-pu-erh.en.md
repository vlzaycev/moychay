Pu-erh is the tea people most often ask about at our tea house – and the first question is usually why two pu-erh cakes on the same shelf look and taste so different. The answer is that there are two kinds: *sheng* (raw) and *shou* (ripe). They start from the same leaf and then take very different paths.

## The same starting point

Both come from Yunnan in southwest China and are made from the leaves of large-leaf tea trees. Fresh leaves are withered, briefly heated to stop oxidation, rolled and dried in the sun. This semi-finished tea is called *maocha*. What happens next decides whether it becomes sheng or shou.

## Sheng: raw pu-erh that ages slowly

For sheng, the maocha is steamed and pressed into cakes or bricks, or kept loose, and then simply left to age. Young sheng is fresh and lively, often floral or fruity, and can be noticeably bitter and astringent. Over years of storage it darkens and becomes smoother and deeper. That is why a young and an aged sheng on our shelves taste like two different teas.

## Shou: ripe pu-erh made in weeks

Shou is made by *wet piling* (*wo dui*): the maocha is piled up, moistened and kept covered for several weeks while controlled fermentation takes place. The method was developed in Yunnan in the early 1970s to give tea some of the depth of aged pu-erh much faster. The result is a dark leaf and a deep red-brown liquor with an earthy, smooth, rounded taste.

## How to tell them apart

| | Sheng (raw) | Shou (ripe) |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Pressed and aged naturally | Wet-piled and fermented for weeks |
| Dry leaf | Green-brown, darker with age | Dark brown to almost black |
| Liquor | Pale yellow to amber | Deep red-brown, almost black |
| Taste | Fresh, floral, can be bitter when young | Earthy, smooth, rounded |
| With age | Changes a lot: deeper and softer | Changes gently: becomes cleaner and calmer |

## Which one to try first

- **You like dark, soft, comforting tea** – start with shou.
- **You like green teas and light oolongs** – try a young sheng.
- **You are curious how time changes tea** – taste a young and an aged sheng side by side.

The easiest way to decide is to taste both. At a [walk-in tasting at our tea house](/tea/) the tea master can brew them one after another.

## A few words on brewing

Both kinds suit gongfu brewing: plenty of leaf, hot water and short infusions. Pressed pu-erh is usually rinsed once before the first cup. Shou forgives very hot water; young sheng can turn bitter if the infusions are too long, so keep them short and lengthen them gradually. If you'd like to see how it is done, the [gongfu cha ceremony](/ceremonies/) is the best lesson.
